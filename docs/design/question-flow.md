# Question flow

## Purpose

- **The quiz is a teaching moment.** The teacher sets the tempo, stops to explain, shows the answer; no clock pressures the teacher.
- **Closure for the whole class, slow readers included.** A child who needed longer to read must not be out of the ranking for it.
- **A broken question is seen before the key is on the wall,** with a moment to pause and discuss before it. AI-made keys are wrong now and then, and the run is the review (backlog, Project).
- **What a tablet shows and what the server counts are one fact.** A student who tapped in time, as far as their tablet showed, and then finds it did not count is disappointed, and the fun is dented for the whole class.
- **Energy for the classes that want it:** points for speed, optional, never a substitute for the teacher's pace.

## Situation

- Plausible assumptions: the teacher reads the question aloud while the class looks at the projector, the choices hidden; a mis-tap is likelier than a tactic.
- Device clocks are not synchronised, and on school Wi-Fi a delivery can take longer than any window that would still be fair. A tablet that is offline still shows the question as open.
- The teacher's pace has no length of its own: a speed bonus measured against it, or against when Countdown was pressed, would reward the class the teacher hurried. The reading happens while the choices are hidden, so a slow reader who listened starts level from the choices appearing.
- Points for speed trade a tie-breaker against a race. In Kahoot a right answer scores from 1,000 points down to about 500 by its speed (Kahoot's "How points work").

### Steering

- The teacher steers from a phone (the Remote), from the projector page itself, or with a presentation clicker that has forward keys only. A hand in flow presses fast; clickers bounce; a double press may be a mistake or a deliberate fast-forward. Plausible assumption: what misleads a hand is the screen moving under it, not the time since the press.
- Switch bounce and a clicker repeating its code are far below 80 ms; double-clicks run about 150–300 ms. On a slow line the screen moves late, which is when it misleads. A button disabled after a step would swallow a double press's second half. A second step across a question's start (closing answering before anyone answered, or the choices before anyone read the question) is never wanted.
- With a quizmaster, Countdown's bar was barely noticeable and relied on the quizmaster announcing it. The same bar carries a Peers room's Countdown (`peers.md`) and the ten seconds after Start, where nobody may announce it.

### Words

- A teacher must be able to say before a press what will change on the wall and on the tablets. With "answers" for both the tiles and what the class tapped, "show the answers" read as showing the key. The teacher screens, the guidance and the format's messages, which the check also hands to the AI, say **choices** for the tiles a question offers, **answers** for what the class tapped, and **solution** for the key (German teachers say *Lösung*). In the code, `answers` is the name of a question's choices.
- As labels, "Reveal" and "Ranking" name a thing the class sees, and worked; "Next question" and "Straight to answering" name a motion, and a fast-forward button found no labels that worked. The wall's bar has room for about 13–15 characters per label, and a noun translates without a verb form, gender or word order.
- An idiom such as "last call" is unknown to many German-speaking teachers; "Countdown" is a loanword in German and Italian.

## Not known

- Whether teachers find Countdown and use it, and whether fifteen seconds is right.
- Whether a deliberate double press is found at all.
- Whether the dashed "on its way" outline is noticed, or needed, on school Wi-Fi.
- Whether 20 s as the speed reference suits questions much longer than that (every slow right answer scores 10).
