# The class and the wall

## Purpose

The class plays on the big screen in a way that is fun, exposes nobody and
stores nothing about them, gets closure from a ranking that slow readers
can follow, and joins inside a minute on tablets nobody has set up. After a question the wall is where a
teaching moment happens: the class sees how it answered, with no names, and
which answers count.

## Situation

The classroom is in `backlog.md`, Project. What bears on this part:

- The children may not read the site's words, or not read at all. Some are
  colour-blind.
- Tablets are shared and school-managed; one may be switched off after a
  lesson and opened for the next class.
- Plausible assumption: reaching the site and typing the code is the slowest
  step, so tablets arrive over about a minute and the early ones wait in the
  lobby. Dressing the avatar fills that wait.
- In testing, people who typed the site's address didn't find the code box
  on the root page.
- A tablet whose battery dies doesn't close its connection, and one left
  lying on a desk stays connected; either can look present to the room.
- The wall is read from across a room, where an avatar is small (about 21 to
  36 px on the who-answered bar): a child mostly sees its colour and
  outline, and the animal barely reads.
- The crowd isn't always a class: a host may run the
  quiz for adults at work, a training room or a team event, on the same
  screens and with the same avatars.

## Letting people in

Purpose: slow tablets in a normal class are never stranded, with no control
for the teacher to find. A quiz under way stays the class's: the join code
stands on the wall, where anyone can pass it on, and outsiders flooding in
would disrupt the lesson. A child whose tablet died gets their own avatar
and score back.

Situation: a teacher may press Start before handing out the tablets, or
start and then fetch the tablet cart; a teacher in front of a class doesn't
look in a menu for a control. Herding a whole class to connect before
teacher and class are familiar with the process can be stressful.

## Sharing a device

Purpose: a class with fewer devices than people, each person with a place,
an avatar and a score of their own.
A nice-to-have: two children on one tablet as one player works without it.

Situation:

- A case on record: 16 people at 6 iPads, three to a device. Phones split
  too.
- A browser makes a click only from a lone touch: with two or three hands
  on the glass, a press is lost while a neighbour's finger is down, and two
  fingers moving pinch the page (seen in Edge's touch emulation; unchecked
  on an iPad). Plausible assumption: a child's deliberate press lifts well
  within a second, and a contact held longer is a hand resting on the glass.
- Children may try to reroll an avatar they don't like by adding and
  closing places.
- Adding or closing a place while a question is up resizes every column
  under the others' fingers.
- A place is a third of a tablet or less and doesn't scroll: long answers
  and long compounds ("Kohlenstoffdioxidkonzentration") don't fit there at
  full size.
- Plausible assumption: children sharing a device sit side by side.
- Apple's school mode for several pupils at one iPad is "Shared iPad"
  ("Geteiltes iPad", "iPad partagé", "iPad condiviso"), plausibly known in
  these schools, and the check speaks of a window shared in a video call;
  "split screen" is the games' word for several players at one screen.
- Plausible assumption, behind the browser keeping the switch: a class short
  of devices is short lesson after lesson.

## How a question fills the screen

Purpose: space used well, a layout that doesn't surprise, and
rules simple enough that the AI building a quiz can be told them, at least
approximately, and shape its questions, answers and pictures to fit.

Situation: a question is read on a 16:9 or 4:3 projector, on laptops and
tablets either way round, and on phones held upright. On the big screen a
question without a picture fits with room to spare; on a phone a long
question competes with its answers for the height; with a picture all three
compete. Answers are often numbers with digit groups ("1 000 000") or with a
unit ("50 Franken"); broken after the number, a tile's first line reads as
the whole answer.

## Who has answered

Purpose: a child wonders whether the tap counted; a mark on the
wall as the answer lands answers that, without showing anyone as missing or
which answer they gave.

## The avatars

Purpose: avatars interesting to the students without wasting everybody's
time or having fights break out. An avatar tells children apart and belongs
to a child. The avatars also let a wider audience use the site, specifically
in a business or training context. The fox in a top hat is the site's mark
(`identity.md`).

Situation:

- Plausible assumption: fights come from something scarce that another
  child holds or could get, and from goods that aren't equal; earned or
  rare items would show performance on the wall.
- At the bar's sizes on the wall, colour, outline and solid blobs survive; a
  thin thing (a staff, a necktie) is 1 to 2 px and gone, white is lost on
  the wall's paper, and a monocle is a grey smudge.
- The answers are marked by a red triangle, blue diamond, yellow circle and
  green square; anything on the wall in an answer's shape and colour reads
  as an answer.
- Plausible assumption, unseen with either audience: an adult at work finds
  a fitting look in the menu, and children don't find the office's things
  dull.

### The end of the dressing

Purpose: whoever is dressing sees
the end coming, and whoever starts has a way to bring the dressing to a
close, with the least surprise for the people on each screen.

Situation:

- In a user test of Peers, Start ended a friend's dressing at once, under
  their fingers. A Quizmaster meets the mirror of it: a class that won't
  stop dressing.
- Some will confuse Ready and Start, even where Ready doesn't exist.
  Plausible assumption: a class of 25 with non-readers can't be waited on
  player by player.
- The one who starts reads; friends on their phones mostly do; children on
  a class's tablets may not, and in a class the teacher says what comes.

## The winners jump

Purpose: a student who was right sees their own avatar celebrate on the
wall. Students asked for it, and in testing preteens were excited to see
their avatar jump.

## The final ranking

Purpose: the end of the quiz and its closure, a celebration of the class
rather than the intermediate ranking with a trophy added. Some teachers
photograph it. Nobody is shown in last place.

## Not known

- A shared device in real hands on a real iPad: palm rejection, whether a
  second tells a resting hand from a press, Safari's four- and five-finger
  gestures (on by default); whether three fit side by side at a 9.7" iPad;
  whether children turn a device when its page turns; how small a child
  still reads an answer and presses a picture in a place. All judged headless only.
- Whether children, and adults at work, dress at all, and whether teachers
  wait for it; whether shapes and things help a child find their avatar
  from the back row or only crowd it.
- Whether Start's remaining time is seen where nobody announces it, and
  whether ten seconds suits both a class and friends who are done.
- Whether the winners read from the back row, and how children take being
  seen not jumping.
- The final ranking on a real projector and in a photograph: festive or
  restless, and whether a child finds their avatar in the crowd.
- Whether the students' buttons need the answer text, or heads stay down.
- Whether the layout reads right on a real projector, and whether answers
  changing between one column and two from question to question surprise
  anyone.
