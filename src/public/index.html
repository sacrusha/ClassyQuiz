About classyQuiz

classyQuiz is an open-source site for playing quizzes: run by a quizmaster for a class or another group on a big screen, played among friends on their own devices, or played alone. It is designed for its user's own AI assistant, any capable one, to make the quiz from what the user brings, such as exercise sheets, notes or just an idea, so that the user's time goes into playing it, or a teacher's into the lesson.

The manual, for AIs and for people who like to get their hands dirty

The user's AI assistant, usually given just this address by someone with material in hand, often a teacher with a lesson coming up, writes the quiz and packages it as a .zip. The user opens the zip on this site (assistants can't), and it is played here. The zip is the same whichever way it is played; the user picks the way after opening it.

Opening the zip shows a check, in the browser, before anything goes to the site's server: the title, the number of questions, the ways to play it, and the questions with their solutions folded away, where a solution can be changed and a question skipped or left uncounted. Whoever will play leaves the questions closed. The ways to play:

  • Solo. One person plays it on their own device, to learn or review: the question, its picture and the choices on that device, the solution right after each answer, the score at the end. Nothing goes to the server, and closing the tab ends it. At the end the player can copy their answers, with the solutions, for their own AI.
  • Peers. Friends play it together, each on their own phone, tablet or laptop, at a table or on a call, and nobody runs it. Whoever opens the room plays too, and has the code for the others. The question and its choices show on every device; once everyone present has answered, the solution, with how many picked each choice; then the ranking; the next question comes once everyone has tapped on. The opener can go on without waiting for someone, change a solution once it shows, and end the quiz.
  • Quizmaster. Someone runs the quiz for a group: a class, a training session, a party, a video call. The questions are on a big screen (a projector, a TV, a shared window), and the players answer on their own phones or tablets, which show the choices only. The quizmaster steers question by question, on the big screen itself or from a second device such as a phone. In a class, the quizmaster is the teacher. The quizmaster doesn't play: their screens take no answers, they decide when the choices come and when the solution shows, and a second device shows them the solution while the others answer.

No accounts; players join a room with its code, or its QR code. A room's quiz, with its players' answers and scores, is deleted when it is ended, or three hours after its last use. The user's lasting copy is the .zip. The browser strips picture metadata before anything goes to the server. For remaining privacy doubts the code is open and an own copy is possible (below); on one, every device opens that copy's own address.

A Quizmaster room starts on its own check (how the screens are arranged, the code for a second device), then the lobby: the join code, a QR code as a shortcut, a display and count of joined avatars, some quick settings, and a start button. The quizmaster then steps through the quiz, question after question: the question with its choices hidden, the choices with answering open, how many picked each choice, perhaps a discussion, the solution, and the updated ranking. Then comes the next question.

A quiz is its user's own material, and this site shares it with nobody beyond the room it is played in. Made from a teacher's sheets for their class, it is both derivative work and teaching material. The site runs on a free plan meant for a few users. The code is public at github.com/sacrusha/ClassyQuiz; an advanced AI assistant can set up a copy on a free Cloudflare account; this site's page /own-copy describes the process.

What informs the shape of the quizzes

Practice, not testing. Nobody is graded. The rankings show avatars, so a low place exposes nobody.

Where the question is read. With a quizmaster, on the big screen, from the back of the room: in a class often by children who read slowly or don't read the language well, with the teacher there to read aloud, and each question first comes up with its choices hidden while it is read. In Solo and Peers, on each player's own device at arm's length, often a phone held upright: everyone reads for themselves, the choices come with the question, and long choices stand one under the other. Either way a choice is a button, not a paragraph.

A teaching moment. With a quizmaster, a question stays up as long as they want, and how many picked each choice shows before the solution, so the group can talk it over. In Peers the spread comes with the solution, in Solo the solution right after each answer. A quiz carries no explanations: a player sees which choice was right, not why.

Used once. A class's quiz is usually made shortly before the lesson, often in the break, and played once, perhaps again in a parallel class.

About fifteen minutes. In a lesson, with pauses to explain, often about fifteen questions. What the user asks for overrides this.

How quizzes are built

A quiz is packaged as one .zip file containing a single text file, quiz.txt (UTF-8), and up to 120 auxiliary files. The text, for a quiz made from a sheet on percentages:

Title: Percentages, week 31

1. What is 10% of 250?
Background: timetable.jpg
+ 25
- 2.5
- 250
- 10

2. A jacket costs 80 francs and is reduced by 25%. What does it cost now?
Image: jacket.png
+ 60 francs
- 55 francs
- 20 francs
- 75 francs

3. Which is more?
+ 50% of 40
- 10% of 120

4. Which quiz should we play next week?
Order: fixed
Image: timetable.jpg
+ Crafts
+ Maths
+ KPop Demon Hunters
+ English

The site shows text as written, without Markdown or other formatting, so mathematical notation is written in ordinary characters: x², ½, √2, 3 × 4, a ≤ b.

The parts

  • Title. The first line, Title: followed by the title. It is shown to the players at the start, and tells whoever opens the zip it's the right quiz.
  • Questions are numbered sequentially 1., 2., …, and may be empty.
  • Choices follow their question, one per line, two to four of them. A right choice's line starts with + , a wrong one's with - . At least one choice must be correct; a player who picks any correct choice scores. The choices are shuffled automatically, so putting the right one first is convenient, not required.

Tags

After a question's text, before its choices.

  • Image: q3.png shows one picture with the question, named by its file in the .zip, where the question is read: on the big screen only with a quizmaster, on each device in Solo and Peers. A wide one is placed between the question and the choices; a square or upright one to the left of a short question and the choices.
  • Order: fixed disables shuffling of choices.
  • Background: forest.jpg is a picture behind the question, cropped to fill the screen the question is read on, typically for atmosphere or context. It lies under a 55% wash of the page colour, rgba(251,248,242,.55), with the text on 90% white cards, so a busy or dark picture stays readable.

Pictures as choices

Title: Fractions and shapes

1. Which picture shows three quarters?
+ Image: pie-3.png
- Image: pie-1.png
- Image: pie-2.png
- Image: pie-4.png

2. Which shape is the mirror image of this one?
Image: l-shape.png
Order: fixed
- Image: l-turned.png
+ Image: l-mirrored.png
- Image: l-same.png
- None of these

A choice's background is red, blue, yellow or green. Consider legibility when a picture is transparent. The site warns when a question's picture choices have different proportions.

Images must be found in the .zip. Names are matched without regard to subfolders or case. PNG, JPEG, GIF, WebP and SVG can be read; HEIC photos from iPhones mostly can't. Images are scaled down: question images to at most 1600 pixels on the longer side, backgrounds to 1920 and choice images to 1200; a smaller image is scaled up. Limits: up to 120 files, and 16 MB for the whole .zip.

For convenience, python verify.py quiz.zip can check format compliance.

Build with the user

The user knows what they need, the AI doesn't. Building a quiz takes a few minutes, just long enough for the user's attention to drift elsewhere. Better to ask for details early, while the user is there, ready to respond, than lose their attention while building something that's only half right.

There are 3 phases to building a quiz:

  1. Defining what gets built. Topic, kind of questions, for whom, how it will be played and whether the user plays too, what language, what size. Information from the user's prompt, attachments, environment, and the AI interacting with the user in order to close gaps.
  2. Building a quiz, or a first draft. This takes 1–3 minutes, and some users will do something else in the meantime, then come back.
  3. Presenting the quiz, and refining it on the user's feedback, unless the user will play it.

When the user will play the quiz

A user who will play the quiz, alone to practise or with friends, plays it without having seen the questions. So the questions, their choices and their solutions stay out of the conversation, and out of anything the AI shows: no draft, no presentation, no preview picture, no question quoted in a summary. What the AI reports instead is what it can without giving a question away: how many questions, the difficulty, how many have pictures, and what of the material is covered and what was left out, named at a grain coarser than the questions, since for a short quiz a topic per question is the question list. Then it hands over the .zip.

What decides it is whether the person in the conversation will be among the players, not the way it is played: a teacher making a quiz for their class to practise with alone reviews it as usual. Nobody looks at the solutions before the game, so the AI checks them itself; a wrong one is caught in play, among friends by whoever opened the room, who can change it once it shows, and alone in the answers the player copies to their AI at the end.

A quizmaster isn't among the players, so a user who says they will host the quiz and play it too is asked which they mean: playing among the others with nobody running it, each on their own device (Peers), where the questions stay out of the conversation; or running it for the group on a big screen (Quizmaster), where the user reviews it as usual.

Presenting

For a user who won't play the quiz, presenting the questions makes the session interactive and allows immediate feedback. Two kinds work well:

  • High density. A dedicated file to visually show the content keeps the conversation focused on the interaction. Zoomed out the user sees by shape what kinds of questions there are, zoomed in they review correctness. Example layout (G→[…] columns sized in pixels or 1*, a share of the rest, more children than columns wrapping into rows; F↓, F→ a stack down or across; ? only when present): 
    Sheet    F↓(Title, G→[1*|1*|1*](Card…))   3000px wide, page #fbf8f2, gap 40, cards in quiz order
Title    "Percentages, week 31 · 4 questions"   bold
Card     F↓(Head, Pictures?, Choices, Tags?)   white, rounded, padding 28
Head     F→(Number, Text)              number white on #a8471a, text wrapping
Pictures F→(Image?, Background?)       300px high, the background washed out and labelled
Choices  G→[1*|1*](Choice…)            with a picture choice G→[1*|1*|1*|1*], 220px high
Choice   F→(Mark, Text | Image)        right: ✓ and a thick #1f8a4c outline; wrong: a thin grey one
Tags     "order fixed"                 small, grey
    Colour can't mark the right choices: on screen, red, blue, yellow and green are the choices' places after the shuffle, so their order on the sheet means nothing either. Optionally python preview.py quiz.zip draws the sheet as review.png and names the characters its font can't draw.
  • High fidelity. The layout page has the big screen's layout for a question as HTML and CSS.

A user who puts all the details into the first prompt usually knows the process and wants step 2 straight away. Such a user may need nothing but the .zip; unless they will play it, they are best asked up front whether they also want a presentation, so they can answer while the first version is being built.

For the questions themselves, what's valued is good questions for the players: easy to understand, with clear right and wrong answers, and pictures that work. A teacher who needs a sheet's exercises taken over one to one says so.

On controversial questions, especially in politics and history — controversy not defined by being mainstream or not, but by the existence of genuine sound arguments pulling very different ways — it tends to work out better to let the user drive.
