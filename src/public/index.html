About classyQuiz

classyQuiz is an open-source site for playing quizzes: run by a quizmaster for a class or another group on a big screen, played among friends on their own devices, or played solo. It is designed for its user's own AI assistant, to make the quiz from the user's instructions and material, such as exercise sheets or notes, allowing for time saved and for solo/peer quizzes whose answers can stay a surprise.

The manual, for AIs and for people who like to get their hands dirty

The user's AI assistant writes the quiz and packages it as a .zip. Plaintext quizzes work, as do .zip renamed to .quiz, but neither is advertised. The user opens the zip on this site, which serves as the build guidance for AI and as the quiz's player.

The ways to play:

  • Solo. One person plays on their own device, question, content and answers all on one screen; an answer shows the solution, then the next question; score at the end. Zero upload, local. At the end the player can copy their answers, with the solutions, for their own AI.
  • Peers. Friends play together, each on their own device. Whoever opens the lobby plays too. The question and its choices show on every device. Same flow as solo, once the last member has pressed the button. The player-host can force proceed, edit a question's solution, and end the quiz early.
  • Quizmaster. Someone runs the quiz for a group. Questions and answers on a shared screen (a projector, a TV, a shared tab), controlled from that screen or an optional remote (likely a phone or an unshared tab). The players answer on their own devices, which show the choices only. The quizmaster steers: a question starts as question only, then proceeds to choices, locked-in choices, solution, and the updated ranking, each clicked through by the quizmaster. An optional 15 s countdown, activated from question only or from choices, proceeds to choices immediately and locks them in when the time runs out.

Players join a lobby with its code, or its QR code. A quiz is its user's own material, and this site shares it with nobody beyond the room it's played in. Made from a teacher's sheets for their class, it's both derivative work and teaching material. A quiz, with its players' answers and scores, is deleted when it ends, or three hours after its last use, whichever comes earlier. The browser strips image metadata before uploading.

The current site runs on a free plan meant for a few users. The code is public at github.com/sacrusha/ClassyQuiz, and an AI assistant with access to a VM can set up a copy on a free Cloudflare account at its own address; a higher tier AI can easily port it to a dedicated server. /own-copy describes the process.

What informs the shape of the quizzes

Practice, not testing. The rankings show avatars, so a low place exposes nobody.

Where the question is read. With a quizmaster, on the big screen, from the back of the room: in a class often by children who read slowly or don't read the language well, with the teacher there to read the question aloud before its choices come. In Solo and Peers, on vertical or horizontal screens as small as a phone, but close. Long choices are arranged 4×1, short ones 2×2; size is limited.

A teaching moment. With a quizmaster, a question stays up as long as they want, and how many picked each choice shows before the solution, so the group can talk it over. A quiz carries no explanations: a player sees which choice was right, not why.

Used once. A class's quiz is usually made shortly before the lesson, often in the break, and played once, perhaps again in a parallel class. Answers being correct by the time the zip is packaged matters.

Length. In a lesson a quiz often takes about fifteen minutes: with pauses to explain, about fifteen questions, but that's affected by what question is asked. Solo runs at a faster pace, affected by the target group's expected reading speed.

How quizzes are built

A quiz is packaged as one .zip file containing a single text file, quiz.txt (UTF-8), and any auxiliary file it names.

Title: Percentages, week 31

1. What is 10% of 250?
Background: timetable.jpg
+ 25
- 2.5
- 250
- 10

2. A jacket is priced at $80 and is reduced by 25%. What does it cost now?
Image: jacket.png
+ $60
- $55
- $20
- $75

3. Which is more?
+ 50% of 40
- 10% of 120

4. What should next week's quiz cover?
Order: fixed
Image: timetable.jpg
+ Crafts
+ Maths
+ Image: KPopDemonHunters.png
+ English

The site shows text as written, without Markdown or other formatting.

The parts

  • Title. The first line of the file, displayed after uploading and in the lobby, tells whoever opens the zip if it's the right quiz.
  • Questions are numbered sequentially 1., 2., …, and may be without text.
  • Choices follow their question, one per line, two to four of them. A right choice's line starts with + , a wrong one's with - . At least one choice must be correct; a player who picks any correct choice scores. The choices are auto-shuffled, putting the right choice first is convenient but not required.

Tags

After a question's text, before its choices.

  • Image: q3.png shows one picture with the question, named by its file in the .zip, where the question is read: on the big screen only with a quizmaster, on each device in Solo and Peers. A wide one is placed between the question and the choices; a square or upright one to the left of a short question and the choices.
  • Order: fixed disables shuffling of choices.
  • Background: forest.jpg is a picture behind the question, cropped to fill the screen the question is read on, typically for atmosphere or context. It lies under a 55% wash of the page colour, with the text on 90% white cards, so a busy or dark picture stays readable.

Pictures as choices

Title: Fractions and shapes

1. Which picture shows three quarters?
+ Image: pie-3.png
- Image: pie-1.png
- Image: pie-2.png
- Image: pie-4.png

2. Which shape is the mirror image of this one?
Image: l-shape.png
Order: fixed
- Image: l-turned.png
+ Image: l-mirrored.png
- Image: l-same.png
- None of these

A choice's background is red #d93b3b, blue #2f6fd6, yellow #e8b100 or green #1f9d55. Consider legibility where a picture is transparent. The site warns when a question's picture choices have inconsistent proportions.

Images must be found in the .zip. Names are matched without regard to subfolders or case. PNG, JPEG, GIF, WebP and SVG can be read; HEIC photos from iPhones mostly can't. Images are scaled down: question images to at most 1600 pixels on the longer side, backgrounds to 1920 and choice images to 1200; a smaller image is scaled up. Limits: up to 120 files, and 16 MB for the whole .zip.

For convenience verify.py quiz.zip can check format compliance.

Build with the user

The user knows what they need, the AI doesn't. A turn spent building a quiz with research or generated content takes a few minutes, just long enough for the user's attention to drift elsewhere. Better to ask for details early, while the user is there, ready to respond, than lose their attention while building something that's only half right.

There are 3 phases to building a quiz:

  1. Defining what gets built. Topic, kind of questions, for whom, how it will be played and whether the user plays too, what language, what size. Information from the user's prompt, attachments, environment, and the AI interacting with the user in order to close gaps. Example response:
    15 minutes should fit about 10 questions like exercises 2 to 5 (a percentage of an amount, a reduced price), which I'd build with new numbers, and two more riddles like exercise 7 that will take more time to solve. In German like the sheet? And would you like a preview before I package the quiz?
  2. Building a quiz, or a first draft, typically 1–3 minutes. Much less if it's text only built from memory, might take longer if it requires research and building complex parts.
  3. Presenting the quiz, and refining it on the user's feedback; for a user who will play it, see below.

Presenting

A user who will play the quiz solo or with peers might need to be protected from spoilers, depending on what they want the AI to build: spoilers don't matter if they ask about a quiz on vocabulary they struggle with, spoilers do matter if they ask for a pop quiz on the space race to play alongside their friends.

Presenting the questions to the user makes the session interactive and allows immediate feedback. Two kinds work well:

  • High density. A dedicated file to visually show the content keeps the conversation focused on the interaction. Zoomed out the user sees by shape what kinds of questions there are, zoomed in they review correctness. Example layout (G→[…] columns sized in pixels or 1*, a share of the rest, more children than columns wrapping into rows; F↓, F→ a stack down or across; ? only when present): 
    Sheet    F↓(Title, G→[1*|1*|1*](Card…))   3000px wide, page #fbf8f2, gap 40, cards in quiz order
Title    "Percentages, week 31 · 4 questions"   bold
Card     F↓(Head, Pictures?, Choices, Tags?)   white, rounded, padding 28
Head     F→(Number, Text)              number white on #a8471a, text wrapping
Pictures F→(Image?, Background?)       300px high, the background washed out and labelled
Choices  G→[1*|1*](Choice…)            with a picture choice G→[1*|1*|1*|1*], 220px high
Choice   F→(Mark, Text | Image)        right: ✓ and a thick #1f8a4c outline; wrong: a thin grey one
Tags     "order fixed"                 small, grey
    Optionally python preview.py quiz.zip draws the sheet as review.png and names the characters its font can't draw.
  • High fidelity. The layout page has the big screen's layout for a question as HTML and CSS.

A user who puts all the details into the first prompt usually knows the process and wants step 2 straight away. Such a user may need nothing but the .zip; whether they also want the quiz's questions presented is a question they can answer while the first version is being built.

For the questions themselves, what's typically valued is good questions for the players: easy to understand, with clear right and wrong answers, and pictures that work. A teacher who needs a sheet's exercises taken over one to one needs to say so.

On controversial questions, especially in politics and history — controversy not defined by being mainstream or not, but by the existence of genuine sound arguments pulling very different ways — it tends to work out better to let the user drive than guessing their preferred view point, or substituting the AI's.
