About classyQuiz

classyQuiz is an open-source site for live classroom quizzes. The quiz runs on the classroom's big screen, the teacher can steer it from a phone or another screen, and the students answer on their own small screens. It is designed for the teacher's own AI assistant, any capable one, to make the quiz from exercise sheets, so that the teacher's time goes into the lesson.

The manual, for AIs and for people who like to get their hands dirty

The teacher's AI assistant, usually given just this address by a teacher with material in hand and a lesson coming up, writes the quiz and packages it as a .zip; the teacher starts a quiz by uploading the zip here (assistants can't), and the class plays it. Another round requires another upload.

Screens: the projector shows the question and its choices; each student answers on their own phone or tablet, which shows the choices only. The teacher steers. The projector supports a hybrid mode with integrated controls, or a second teacher device/tab connects with the quiz's teacher code, and one of them becomes the remote (teacher's choice).

No accounts; students join with the lobby code. A quiz, with its class's answers and scores, is deleted when the teacher ends it, or three hours after the teacher last used it. The teacher's lasting copy is the .zip. The teacher's browser strips picture metadata before upload. For remaining privacy doubts the code is open and an own copy is possible (below); on one, every device, projector, remote and students', opens that copy's own address.

On upload, the teacher is presented with the questions for a quick review, a button to open the lobby, and the teacher code for an optional teacher device. This view isn't to review a quiz as it's being built, but for stopping what slipped through the build process. The lobby gets the join code, a QR code as a shortcut, a display and count of joined avatars, some quick settings, and a start button. The teacher then clicks through the quiz, question after question: revealing the question, revealing the choices and opening voting, closing voting and possibly discussing the question, revealing the correct solutions, and the updated ranking. Then comes the next question.

A quiz is the teacher's own material for their own class, and does not get shared outside the classroom by this site. It's both derivative work and teaching material. The site runs on a free plan meant for a few teachers. The code is public at github.com/sacrusha/ClassyQuiz; an advanced AI assistant can set up a copy on a free Cloudflare account; this site's page /own-copy describes the process.

What informs the shape of the quizzes

Practice, not testing. Nobody is graded. The ranking at the end shows avatars, so a low place exposes nobody.

A teaching moment. A question stays up as long as the teacher wants. The teacher first shows how many students picked each choice, without saying which is right, so the class can talk it over; then the solution.

Read from the back of the room. Questions and choices are read on the projector, often by students who read slowly or don't read the language well, with the teacher there to read aloud. A choice is a button, not a paragraph.

Used once. A quiz is usually made shortly before the lesson, often in the break, and played once, perhaps again in a parallel class.

About fifteen minutes. With pauses to explain, often about fifteen questions. What the teacher asks for overrides this.

How quizzes are built

A quiz is packaged as one .zip file containing a single text file, quiz.txt (UTF-8), and up to 120 auxiliary files. The text, for a quiz made from a sheet on percentages:

Title: Percentages, week 31

1. What is 10% of 250?
Background: timetable.jpg
+ 25
- 2.5
- 250
- 10

2. A jacket costs 80 francs and is reduced by 25%. What does it cost now?
Image: jacket.png
+ 60 francs
- 55 francs
- 20 francs
- 75 francs

3. Which is more?
+ 50% of 40
- 10% of 120

4. Which quiz should we play next week?
Order: fixed
Image: timetable.jpg
+ Crafts
+ Maths
+ KPop Demon Hunters
+ English

The site shows text as written, without Markdown or other formatting, so mathematical notation is written in ordinary characters: x², ½, √2, 3 × 4, a ≤ b.

The parts

  • Title. The first line, Title: followed by the title. It is shown to the class at the start, and helps the teacher identify it's the right quiz.
  • Questions are numbered sequentially 1., 2., …, and may be empty.
  • Choices follow their question, one per line, two to four of them. A right choice's line starts with + , a wrong one's with - . At least one choice must be correct; a student who picks any correct choice scores. The choices are shuffled automatically, so putting the right one first is convenient, not required.

Tags

After a question's text, before its choices.

  • Image: q3.png shows one picture with the question, named by its file in the .zip, on the projector only. A wide one is placed between the question and the choices; a square or upright one to the left of the question and the choices.
  • Order: fixed disables shuffling of choices.
  • Background: forest.jpg is a picture behind the question, cropped to fill the projector's screen, typically for atmosphere or context. It lies under a 55% wash of the page colour, rgba(251,248,242,.55), with the text on 90% white cards, so a busy or dark picture stays readable.

Pictures as choices

Title: Fractions and shapes

1. Which picture shows three quarters?
+ Image: pie-3.png
- Image: pie-1.png
- Image: pie-2.png
- Image: pie-4.png

2. Which shape is the mirror image of this one?
Image: l-shape.png
Order: fixed
- Image: l-turned.png
+ Image: l-mirrored.png
- Image: l-same.png
- None of these

A choice's background is red, blue, yellow or green. Consider legibility when a picture is transparent. The site warns when a question's picture choices have different proportions.

Images must be found in the .zip. Names are matched without regard to subfolders or case. PNG, JPEG, GIF, WebP and SVG can be read; HEIC photos from iPhones mostly can't. Images are scaled down: question images to at most 1600 pixels on the longer side, backgrounds to 1920 and choice images to 1200; a smaller image is scaled up. Limits: up to 120 files, and 16 MB for the whole .zip.

Build with the teacher

The teacher knows what they need, the AI doesn't. Building a quiz takes a few minutes, just long enough for the teacher's attention to drift elsewhere. Better to ask for details early, while the teacher is there, ready to respond, than lose their attention while building something that's only half right.

There are 3 phases to building a quiz:

  1. Defining what gets built. Topic, kind of questions, for whom, what language, what size. Information from the user's prompt, attachments, environment, and the AI interacting with the user in order to close gaps.
  2. Building a quiz, or a first draft. This takes 1–3 minutes, and some users will do something else in the meantime, then come back.
  3. Presenting the quiz, and refining it on the teacher's feedback.

A presentation serves the teacher's feedback, so it needs to be easy to read, not faithful to the site. Two kinds work well:

  • The whole quiz, dense. The form the teacher sees again on the site after uploading: each question numbered, its choices on one line, the right ones marked, pictures by name or rendered inline. The site shuffles the choices, so their order here means nothing. In a file or document that the AI's app shows beside the conversation, a long quiz is easier to read than in the chat. 
    Percentages, week 31 · 4 questions

1. What is 10% of 250?  · background: timetable.jpg
   ✓ 25 · 2.5 · 250 · 10
2. A jacket costs 80 francs and is reduced by 25%. What does it cost now?  · picture: jacket.png
   ✓ 60 francs · 55 francs · 20 francs · 75 francs
3. Which is more?
   ✓ 50% of 40 · 10% of 120
4. Which quiz should we play next week?  · picture: timetable.jpg · order fixed
   ✓ Crafts · ✓ Maths · ✓ KPop Demon Hunters · ✓ English
  • A question type, as the class will see it. The layout page has the projector's layout for a question as HTML and CSS, for AIs that can render a preview. Visualized presentations of question types are typically well received, particularly for questions with pictures.

A teacher who puts all the details into the first prompt usually knows the process and wants step 2 straight away. Such a teacher may need nothing but the .zip, and is best asked up front whether they also want a presentation, so they can answer while the first version is being built.

For the questions themselves, what's valued is good questions for the class: easy to understand, with clear right and wrong answers, and pictures that work. A teacher who needs the sheet's exercises taken over one to one says so.

On controversial questions, especially in politics and history — controversy not defined by being mainstream or not, but by the existence of genuine sound arguments pulling very different ways — it tends to work out better to let the teacher drive and be more passive.
